A collaborative session joining ESPhil’s AI Session seminar and the Open Science Community Rotterdam

A collaborative session joining ESPhil’s AI Session seminar and the Open Science Community Rotterdam

Microsoft, our main office software provider, has implemented GenAI tools into all of its interfaces and applications, enhancing its monopoly on our workflow possibilities and research styles/methods/etc. As academic institutions become increasingly dependent on the commercial domain (driven by very different values than those we normally associate with academia and science), critical questions come at us fast: questions about research autonomy, about data ethics, and about principles of open science.

We’d like to invite all who are interested to a discussion around the stakes of migrating away from Microsoft, from philosophical and technical perspectives, and in the name of open research and academic integrity.

  • Date: January 14th 2026
  • Time: 14:30 to 16:00
  • Location: Glasbak (ESPhil meeting room, Bayle building 5th floor)

Why?
The question of where research data is stored and who controls it has become increasingly urgent as universities and even governmental institutions entrust sensitive information to private digital infrastructure (see, e.g., the recent DigiD news https://www.bnr.nl/nieuws/economie/10587368/zorgen-binnen-de-overheid-nederlandse-cloudaanbieder-ook-in-amerikaanse-handen). Platform-dependence; lock-in issues; the monopolization of education; GDPR compliance; etc., are but the tip of the iceberg, one which melts fast and brings a hoard of sociopolitical questions with it. And clearly, as we have been experiencing: feature changes force our academic work to be perpetually reactive to Microsoft’s market strategy moves.

GenAI
The embedding of GenAI “assistants” into the daily workflows of staff/students puts us in situations where the boundaries between doing research and being the subject of research by a private company become blurred. Add to this that the homogenization of thought (and effect of GenAI) is probably exactly the opposite of what a university should desire from its staff and students: we are here to create knowledge, not regurgitate patterns from the past (what GenAI does).

Open Science
Microsoft’s products are tools designed for general corporate use rather than specialized academic inquiry, and the reliance on proprietary platforms contradicts the principles of open science that we should uphold. Being dependent on Microsoft creates additional barriers to reproducibility, transparency, interoperability, etc., and these problems fragment the scientific community along techno-commercial lines based on software infrastructure affordances and access rather than intellectual production.

Ethical concerns
Beyond the scientific, practical and technical: we need to consider Microsoft’s significant carbon footprint; the concentration of global knowledge infrastructures in the hands of a few corporations from wealthy nations; and importantly in recent times: the company’s involvement in war crimes (and therefore the use of our research for these purposes). See for example our colleagues’ at Radboud reactions to this: https://www.cs.ru.nl/~hiemstra/radboud-against-microsoft/.

During our discussion we will consider alternatives (e.g., Nextcloud) that offer more transparency, customization, and distributed/community control of academic/office tools.

We hope to see you on the 14th of January to discuss these issues together.
